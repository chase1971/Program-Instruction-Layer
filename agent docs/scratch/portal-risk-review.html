Student portal & teacher console

Things that could go wrong, why each one matters, and what to do about it.

If you only do three things

  1. Raise the 500-attempt ceiling in the teacher console. Right now it can only see your most recent 500 attempts, and it drops the oldest ones silently. This one is already counting down on its own.
  2. Stop the "wrong link" screen from printing the other student's code. It currently shows one student's private code to whoever is holding the device. That's handing out the key.
  3. Close the back door that lets a browser write any score it wants. Until that's shut, server-side grading isn't actually protecting anything, so nothing here could honestly be worth points.

1. Your data quietly going wrong

This is the category you said you were most worried about, and it's where the real problems are. None of these throw an error. Everything looks like it worked.

Fix firstHappens on its own

The console can only see your newest 500 attempts — and silently forgets the rest

When Macro App loads student progress, it asks the database for attempts newest first, stop at 500. That one number covers everything at once: all your sections, all your apps, finished work and half-finished work alike.

Why it's bad: once you cross 500, the console starts dropping the oldest rows — September's work goes first. There's no warning, no error, no "showing 500 of 700." The grid just quietly stops knowing about early-semester work, and you'd have no reason to suspect it. Thirty students times one exit ticket is thirty rows. Three sections and half a dozen tickets in, you're at the ceiling. The data is still safe in the database — it's the console's view of it that goes blind.

What to do

Two parts. Short term: raise the limit and filter the request down to the section you're actually looking at, so you're not spending the budget on other classes. Proper fix: page through the results instead of capping them, and show a row count so a truncation could never be invisible again. There's a second, tighter cap of 200 on the completed-attempts list that needs the same treatment.

Macro App/electron-app/student-progress-io.js:200 (and the 200-row query below it)

Fix firstHappens on its own

A student who reopens a finished quiz looks like they never did it

Your response grid shows each student's most recent attempt. If a student submits their exit ticket, then comes back later to look at it again and taps a single answer, that tap starts a brand-new, empty attempt. That empty one is now the most recent — so it's the one the grid shows.

Why it's bad: their row goes blank and your "completed" count drops by one. Their real answers are still in the database, perfectly intact, but the screen says they didn't do the work. If you were grading off that screen you'd give a zero to someone who finished — and the student would have no idea they'd done anything.

What to do

When picking which attempt to show, prefer the most recent completed one, and only fall back to an unfinished attempt if there's no completed one at all. Small change in a single place, and it fixes the whole grid at once.

Macro App/renderer/src/hooks/teacher-console/useExitTicketResponseReport.ts:52

Worth fixingHappens on its own

A student with bad wifi can get their answers recorded twice

Submitting happens in two steps: save the answers, then mark the attempt finished. If the first step works and the second one times out, the student sees an error and presses Finish again — which saves the whole set of answers a second time. Nothing stops a duplicate.

Why it's bad: the student's own grade history starts showing 26 questions for a 13-question tutorial, which is confusing enough on its own. The quieter problem is that it only happens to students with unreliable connections. So when you eventually go looking at where students stall, your per-question data is doubled for exactly one group of kids — the ones on bad phones. That's a slanted dataset, and you'd never spot it by looking at it.

What to do

Clear out any previously saved answers for that attempt before writing the new set, which is what the newer exit-ticket path already does correctly. Belt and braces: add a rule in the database saying one attempt can't hold two answers for the same question, so a duplicate becomes impossible rather than merely unlikely.

student-portal/src/services/attemptSubmission.ts:57

Worth fixingHappens on its own

Fixing a typo in an answer key doesn't re-grade anyone

You can edit a published exit ticket, including which option is the correct one. But nothing re-marks the attempts that already came in.

Why it's bad: the first time you mistype an answer key and catch it after twenty students have submitted, those twenty keep the wrong result permanently — and there's no button to fix it. You'd be hand-correcting in your gradebook and hoping you remembered everyone. There's a sharper version of this too: editing a published ticket lets you rewrite the wording of the options while keeping everything else in place. Do that, and last week's stored answers now point at options that say something different. The old data stops meaning anything, and nothing flags it.

What to do

Add a "re-grade this ticket" action that re-marks the existing attempts against the current key — it's the same logic as grading, just pointed at answers you already have. And make the editor refuse to change option wording once a student has submitted. Renaming the title or reordering questions is harmless; changing what an option says isn't.

student-session-kit/supabase/migrations/016_update_exit_ticket.sql

Worth fixingOnly when you click it

"Revoke code" deletes the student — and orphans everything they ever submitted

Revoking a student's code doesn't switch the code off, it deletes the student record. Their submitted attempts survive, but the link back to who submitted them is wiped. Their login history is deleted outright.

Why it's bad: the one time you'd reach for this is mid-semester — a student's code got passed around, or they lost their device and need a fresh link. Do it, and every score they had already earned becomes unattributable. Not recoverable either: the connection is simply gone. Your own pipeline doc says "deactivate dropped students, don't delete them" — the button does the opposite of what you'd already decided.

Sitting right next to it: "reset attempts" has no per-quiz option. It deletes every attempt that student ever made. If you reach for it meaning "let them retake today's warm-up," you've wiped their semester.

What to do

Add an "active" flag to students and have revoke flip that instead of deleting — the code stops working, the history stays attached, and you can undo it. Then give reset-attempts a quiz selector, with "all quizzes" as a deliberate extra choice rather than the only behaviour.

2. What a student might see break

Each of these produces either an email to you or a quiet wrong impression. None of them need anyone doing anything clever.

Fix firstHappens on its own

The "that's not your link" screen shows the other student's code in big bold letters

When a device is already locked to one student and someone opens a different link on it, the portal blocks them — and displays "Your code: XXXXXX" in large type, meaning the code belonging to whoever set the device up.

Why it's bad: picture a campus lab machine, or one student borrowing another's phone for a minute. Student B opens their own link, gets blocked, and the screen hands them student A's code — which is the entire login. Now B can sign in as A from their own phone, read A's grades, and submit work in A's name. The feature built to stop link-sharing is the thing that leaks the key. On top of that there's no way out of the screen: the only button signs you in as the other student.

What to do

Don't print the code. Say "this device is already signed in as someone else" and offer two big buttons: Continue as the student already signed in, and This isn't me — sign this device out, where the second clears the device so the next student can use their own link. That turns a dead end into a shared-computer feature and stops the leak at the same time.

student-portal/src/app/components/PortalGate.tsx:30

Worth fixingHappens on its own

The cheating log blames the wrong student

When that mismatch happens it gets recorded — but it's filed under the student the device belongs to, with the arriving student listed as the other party.

Why it's bad: in the shared-computer case that's exactly backwards. The record reads "A went after B's link," when what actually happened is B walked up to A's browser. A did nothing. If you ever looked at that log to settle a question about academic honesty, you'd be looking at an accusation pointed at the wrong person — stored in the database as though it were a fact.

What to do

Record it as a neutral event instead of an attribution: "this device, signed in as A, had B's link opened on it." Same two names, no implied culprit. And in the console, label that column something like "link mix-ups" rather than anything that reads as cheating, because a shared machine is by far the most likely cause.

student-portal/src/hooks/useStudentIdentity.ts, migration 014

Worth fixingHappens on its own

A one-time quiz can hang on a blank loading screen forever

For a quiz set to "one attempt only," the portal checks whether the student has already done it before letting them start. If that check fails — a dropped connection, a hiccup, anything — the error isn't caught. The screen sits on "loading" and never moves.

Why it's bad: the student can't start, can't retry, and has nothing to tell you except "it didn't work." Reloading might fix it or might not. This is the kind of thing that generates five confused messages during one class period and that you can never reproduce afterwards.

What to do

Catch the failure and show a real message with a retry button. Then decide the safe default: I'd let them into the quiz if the check can't complete, since the database refuses a second submission anyway. Better a student who starts and gets politely stopped than a student staring at a dead screen.

student-portal/src/features/generic-quiz/useGenericQuiz.ts:99

Worth fixingHappens on its own

Reopening a finished quiz tells the student they scored 0 of 0

If a student goes back into a one-attempt quiz they already completed, the portal knows they're done — it passes along a flag saying so. The screen ignores that flag completely and falls through to the normal results display, which reads "Quiz complete — 0 of 0."

Why it's bad: on a graded quiz that looks exactly like a zero. The student panics, emails you, and you have to go check the database to reassure them. On an ungraded exit ticket it happens to land on friendly wording by accident, which is probably why nobody has reported it yet.

What to do

Small one. Use the flag that's already being handed over, and show "You've already completed this — your answers are saved," with their real score if it's graded. Ten minutes of work.

student-portal/src/features/generic-quiz/GenericQuizResult.tsx:18

Know about itHappens on its own

Phones forget who the student is, and two tabs fight each other

Two separate things with one cause — who the student is lives only in that browser's local storage.

Why it's bad: iPhones clear that storage for sites you haven't visited in about a week. So a student who skips seven days gets silently signed out and needs the original email again — which, by then, plenty of them will have deleted. Same story if they first opened the link inside the D2L app's built-in browser and later try regular Safari: different storage, not signed in. Separately, if a student has the same quiz open in two tabs or on two devices, both share a single saved-progress record and the last one to save wins — so a stale tab can overwrite newer answers.

What to do

Accept the logouts, but make recovery not involve you: on the "you need your class link" screen, let them type their six-character code by hand. It's the same credential, it's short, and it turns a support email into five seconds of typing. For the two-tabs problem, have a save that arrives with older progress than what's already stored get refused instead of applied.

3. Ways someone could game it

Ordered by how much effort it takes. The first one decides whether any of this can ever be worth points.

Fix firstNeeds someone trying

A browser can still write any score it likes — which makes server-side grading decorative

You built proper server-side grading, where the answer key never leaves the database. But the older, wide-open path from the original build is still switched on next to it: a browser is allowed to create an attempt and mark it finished with whatever score it names. Nothing checks that the attempt belongs to the person sending it, and nothing checks the score is plausible.

Why it's bad: it means the careful version isn't actually protecting anything. A student who wants a 10/10 doesn't need to beat the grading function, they can walk around it — and the same move skips your "one attempt only" setting and the class check. It also works in reverse: the same door lets someone write a zero into a classmate's record, which is sabotage rather than cheating, and would look completely genuine to you. Your docs list closing this as optional cleanup. It isn't — it's the difference between "worth points" being honest or not.

What to do

Turn off the two leftover permissions that let a browser write attempt rows directly, and stop letting browsers call the "mark this finished" function at all. Everything in the exit-ticket path already goes through proper grading, so this should be quiet — the Matrix tutorial is the one thing to check first, since it still submits the old way and would need moving over.

migrations 001 (the two "with check (true)" policies) and 004 (finish_attempt)

Worth fixingA clever student could

Unlimited retries plus per-question feedback adds up to the answer key

New quizzes default to unlimited attempts, and after each submission the portal tells the student which individual questions they got right. The console shows you their most recent attempt.

Why it's bad: a student doesn't need to hack anything — they submit, see which four they missed, change those, and submit again. Four rounds on a ten-question, four-option quiz is enough to reach full marks, and the only attempt you'd see is the perfect one. The work you did to keep the answer key on the server protects the key, but it doesn't slow this down at all.

What to do

For anything graded, set it to one attempt when you publish — and consider making one attempt the default, so the safe choice is what happens when you don't think about it. If you want retries for practice, hold the per-question breakdown back until the last attempt, or show only the total. Worth saying: that breakdown is genuinely good for learning, so this is a real trade-off per assignment rather than a bug to stamp out.

Worth fixingNeeds someone curious

Your personal test code is sitting inside the published website

CHASE1 is written into the portal's code, which means it ships inside the JavaScript file every student's browser downloads. Anyone who opens developer tools, or just searches the page source for likely words, can read it.

Why it's bad: that code is deliberately special — it skips the class check and skips the one-device lock. So it's a universal key to every section's apps, published on the open internet, and short enough to guess if someone felt lucky. And because everyone using it shares one identity, any work submitted under it piles onto the same record and muddies whatever you were testing.

What to do

Take it out of the code and make it an ordinary student row in the database that happens to be enrolled in every section — then it's a secret you hold rather than one you ship. If you want a true bypass for testing, drive it off something only you can set on your own machine, so it can't exist in the public bundle at all.

student-portal/src/config/portalAccess.ts (and the built dist bundle)

Low priorityNeeds real effort

Codes use the weak kind of randomness, and nobody's watching for guessing

Two small things stacked together. The codes come from the everyday random-number generator rather than the cryptographic one. And the sign-in function can be called from anywhere on the internet as many times as someone likes, with failed guesses leaving no trace — only successes get logged.

Why it's bad: a billion possible codes is plenty against someone typing guesses by hand. But the everyday generator is predictable if you can see a few of its outputs, and a whole section's codes get made in one run — so in principle one leaked code could help work out the others. More practically, nothing would tell you if someone were scanning for valid codes; you'd find out when unexplained attempts showed up. Honestly: this is the least likely thing on the page to ever happen to you. It's here because the fix is nearly free.

What to do

Switch the code generator to the cryptographic random function — a one-line change that makes the prediction angle impossible rather than merely unlikely. If you ever want the monitoring half, log failed code attempts too, so a scan would show up as a wall of misses instead of nothing.

student-portal/scripts/lib/roster-codes.mjs:65

Know about itWaiting to happen

A publishing mismatch can send a quiz to nobody and still say "published"

A quiz gets attached to a class by matching the course name and the term. The console always sends the term as the word "current", while the roster side uses whatever term is in the course file and only falls back to "current." The course-name match is also case-sensitive.

Why it's bad: today those happen to agree, so it works. But the moment a course file carries a real term like "Fall 2026," or a course name differs by a single capital letter, the publish quietly creates a second, empty class and files the quiz there. Every student gets "not available for this class," while your console reports a successful publish. You'd be debugging the portal when the problem is one mismatched word.

What to do

Publish to the class by its database id — the one the roster sync already knows — instead of re-matching on name and term. Failing that, have publishing refuse to invent a new class: if nothing matches, stop and say so. Either way it becomes a loud error instead of a silent one.

4. How to talk about this with your college

Call it "pseudonymised," not "anonymous" — it's a stronger position, not a weaker one

Your design is genuinely good and I'd keep it exactly as it is. But I'd change how you describe it. Right now the claim is that nothing identifies a student. The accurate version is: no student name or email is ever stored in the cloud, and the only list connecting codes to people lives on your machine.

Why the wording matters: "anonymous" collapses the moment anyone points out that you can obviously tell who's who — you have the list, that's the whole point of it. Under FERPA, data you can re-link is still a student record. But "no names in the cloud, and the key never leaves my office computer" is both true and exactly what a privacy reviewer wants to hear. Claiming the stronger thing and getting corrected is worse than claiming the accurate thing first, especially with a committee that's already nervous about technology.

Two things have quietly grown past the original description, worth knowing before someone asks. The login log now records a timestamp every time a student signs in or opens an app — combined with your local list, that's an attendance-and-behaviour record, not just scores, and nothing deletes it, so it accumulates forever. And the "wrong link" entries are stored allegations about two identifiable students, which the earlier item explains can point at the wrong one.

What to do

Write a short retention rule and actually implement it — something like "login events are deleted after 90 days, scores at the end of the following term." That's one small scheduled cleanup. Being able to say "here's my retention policy" without being asked does more for you with a cautious committee than any technical detail, and it caps how much behavioural history exists to worry about in the first place. Keep it on your existing FERPA notes rather than starting a new doc.

5. Things I checked that are fine

  • No student names or emails anywhere in the cloud database. I went looking. The design holds.
  • Answer keys don't reach the browser on the newer exit-ticket path — the function that hands out questions deliberately leaves the correct answer behind.
  • Made-up codes write nothing. A stranger typing a random code gets turned away, and no record is created for them.
  • Sign-in fails closed. If the database is unreachable, the portal blocks rather than letting someone in.
  • The app-access toggle fails closed too — if the check errors out, the student is denied rather than let through.
  • Your service key is encrypted on disk and never goes near a browser.
  • The "most recent attempt" sorting is correct. I suspected an off-by-one there and it's genuinely right.
  • Costs are not a risk at your scale. Nothing here pushes Supabase off the free tier, and Netlify spend is about how often you deploy, not how much students use it.
